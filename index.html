Temporal controllability benchmark · preview

When, not just whata timing benchmark for music

Music generators sound impressive, but for anything synced to picture the music has to hit its mark on time. No existing benchmark measures that. This is the first to test temporal controllability: whether a system places an instructed musical event within a fraction of a second of when you asked for it. We put four leading systems through it alongside taan.ai, the system built to win on this axis.

The gap

Models nail what to play. they miss when.

Text-to-music systems produce polished audio in seconds, but applications that sync music to picture (film, ads, games, short-form video) need events to land within a few hundred milliseconds. "Bring in the drums at the sixth second" only works if the drums actually arrive at the sixth second. Existing evaluation measures fidelity and overall prompt match. None asks whether the music does the right thing at the right time.

What it measures

Did the instructed event happen, on time?

Each prompt asks for a specific musical event at a specific moment. We score whether it lands within a tolerance of the requested timestamp, across three families of instruction:

Results

Timing control is largely absent today. taan changes that.

Across four widely used systems, tempo instructions are usually followed, but timed events land within ±500 ms only a fraction of the time. Models place events near the requested moment, not at it. taan.ai, built timing-first, delivers the large majority of instructed events on time.

Music quality

Timing control, without giving up on sound.

Controllability only counts if the music still sounds good. We score audio fidelity with Fréchet Audio Distance (FAD), a standard generative-audio metric where lower means closer to real music. Each clip is loudness-normalized to −14 LUFS, trimmed to 30 s, and turned into VGGish features, then compared against a fixed real-music reference corpus (GTZAN) with 95% confidence intervals over clips. taan is early on fidelity by design; that is a deliberate trade for timing precision none of the others have, and quality is the fastest-moving part of the stack.

Fréchet Audio Distance vs real music (lower is better). Lyria is closest to real music at 2.6; the commercial field spans 2.6 to 3.5. taan sits at 4.8 today, a deliberate early trade for the timing precision none of the others have, and fidelity is the fastest-moving part of the stack.
How we measure

A reproducible, self-calibrated pipeline.

Every score comes from a deterministic signal-processing pipeline: prompts are turned into machine-checkable events, audio is generated, each event is routed to a purpose-built detector, and a candidate is credited only if it clears a cascade of presence checks, so dense arrangements cannot score by luck. The pipeline's own localization error is measured against human labels and published, so model differences are never confused with measurement noise.

Prompts to generation to analysis to scoring to results, all resting on human calibration and published per-family measurement error. Full methodology is reserved for the paper.
The headline number measures every system through the same blind separation front end, which is trained on real recordings. On synthesized instruments that front end can miss an event that is actually present, so taan's real timing rate is higher than the blind number shows: scored on taan's own rendered stems, timing compliance is about 76%. We report the blind number as the fair cross-system comparison and the stem number as an upper bound, never mixing the two.
